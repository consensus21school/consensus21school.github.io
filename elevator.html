01

What is Consensus 21?

Consensus 21 works the way Bitcoin works — but for school. No single company owns it. Instead, every campus (a node) runs the same open playbook, and the playbook only changes when the community agrees. A great idea at one node can spread to every node on Earth.

Simply put

It's a team of schools around the world that all share one open rulebook — and anyone in the community can suggest making the rulebook better.

The node

One small campus

A Consensus 21 school is deliberately small — a tight community of learners, families, educators and local businesses. Small enough that everyone is known, connected to a worldwide network of peers.

The protocol

One shared playbook

Every node runs the Open Learning Protocol — an open, published standard for how learning, assessment, funds and governance work. Open means anyone can read it, verify it, or start a node.

The upgrade path

SIPs keep it alive

School Improvement Proposals let any learner, parent or community member pitch a change. Approved SIPs are merged into the living curriculum — so the school upgrades itself, forever.

02

The K–21 journey

Most schools end with a certificate and a "good luck." Consensus 21 ends with capital, a company, and a community. The journey runs in five stages — and the last one is the point of all the others.

Simply put

You spend ages 5–16 learning and practising with real money, graduate at 16, then spend ages 16–21 building your own business with everyone's help.

Ages 5–9

Explore

Reading, numbers, making and play. First savings, first sats, first ideas. Curiosity is the curriculum.

Ages 10–13

Build skills

Mastery-based academics at each learner's own pace, plus hands-on projects, coding, and real financial literacy: earn, save, spend, verify.

Ages 14–16

Launch prep

Learners run micro-businesses, keep real books, pitch SIPs, and design the venture they'll launch at graduation.

Age 16

Graduation 🔓

The timelock opens. Credentials plus seed capital, released directly to the learner. Not the end of school — the start of the venture.

Ages 16–21

Venture years

Five years of fostered founding: mentors, workspace at the node, advisors, and a community of first customers. At 21, a young adult with a five-year-old business.

03

The timelock: a piggy bank with a clock instead of a key

From the day a learner enrols, contributions are set aside into their personal fund. The fund is secured with a Bitcoin timelock — a rule written into the money itself that says "this cannot be spent before graduation." Not by the school. Not by parents. Not by a bank. Nobody.

Simply put

Money is saved for you every year you're at school, and the maths of Bitcoin keeps it locked until the day you graduate — then it's yours, to start something real.

Why lock it?

It removes trust

A promise can be broken; a timelock can't. The learner can verify their fund exists and verify exactly when it opens — years in advance.

Why age 16?

Ready, not waiting

By 16, a Consensus 21 learner has managed money, run micro-businesses and kept books for years. Capital arrives when energy and preparation peak — not after years of queues and debt.

Why to the learner?

Skin in the game

The fund is released to the graduate, not to an institution. It's their venture, their decisions, their responsibility — with the community as mentors, not gatekeepers.

Like Alpha School — for Bitcoiners

Alpha School proved that mastery academics can be compressed into a couple of focused hours a day, freeing afternoons for real-world skills. Consensus 21 shares that DNA — then adds three things Alpha doesn't: financial literacy and Bitcoin as a core pillar, a timelocked graduation fund, and an open protocol nobody owns.

Simply put

Same big idea — learn fast in the morning, do real things in the afternoon — but here the "real things" include money you can hold, a business you will own, and a school anyone can copy.

05

SIPs: how the school upgrades itself

Bitcoin improves through BIPs — Bitcoin Improvement Proposals. Consensus 21 improves through SIPs — School Improvement Proposals. Anyone can raise one: a seven-year-old obsessed with beekeeping, a parent who's a paramedic, a local welder, a visiting founder. If the community backs it, it becomes part of the living curriculum at that node — and any node in the network can adopt it.

Simply put

If you're passionate about something, you get up and give a TED-style talk about it. If people love it and three of the six key-holders sign, your idea becomes part of school. Yes — even if you're eight.

SIP-01 · PITCH

The talk

Any learner or community member gives a short, TED-style presentation on what they're passionate about and why the school should teach or adopt it.

SIP-02 · REVIEW

Open discussion

The community questions, tests and refines the idea in the open. Who will run it? What does it cost? How do we know it worked?

SIP-03 · SIGN

3-of-6 multisig

Six key-holders drawn from the school community hold signing keys. Any three signatures approve the proposal — no single person can force or block a change alone.

SIP-04 · MERGE

Into the curriculum

The approved SIP is merged into the node's dynamic curriculum — versioned like software, visible to every node in the network, free for any node to adopt.

Example: A learner in Mornington Peninsula pitches SIP-0042: "Oyster reef restoration as marine science". It's reviewed, three keys sign, and it merges. A month later the El Salvador node — on its own coastline — adopts the same SIP. That's interoperability: one kid's passion becomes curriculum on two continents.
